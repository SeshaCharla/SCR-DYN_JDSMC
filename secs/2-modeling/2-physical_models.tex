\subsection{Discrete Nonlinear Dynamics}

The system is modelled as a discrete plug-flow system. Within each sampling period $t_s$, there are $n$ residence
times. At each residence time, a fresh parcel of exhaust gas enters and reacts with the catalyst surface. We now
discretize each governing equation by tracking what happens across these $n$ sub-steps.

\subsubsection{Surface $\ammonia$ Dynamics}
We first derive the unbounded surface dynamics assuming no saturation constraints, then introduce the switching function
that enforces the physical bounds $0 \leq \sigma \leq \Gamma$.

At each sub-step (residence time) $i$, molar conservation on the catalyst surface gives:
\begin{align}
    \con{\ammonia}^{ads}(k{+}(i{+}1)\tau) = \con{\ammonia}^{ads}(k{+}i\tau)
        + \tau\,\dot{\con{\ammonia}}^{ads}(k{+}i\tau)
\end{align}
where the rate is treated as constant within each residence time. The gaseous inputs
($\con{\ammonia}^{in}$, $\con{\nox}^{in}$) are held constant (ZOH) across the entire sample, so only the surface
concentration changes sub-step to sub-step. Summing telescopically from $i = 0$ to $n{-}1$ (all intermediate terms
cancel):
\begin{multline}
    \con{\ammonia}^{ads}(k{+}1) - \con{\ammonia}^{ads}(k) = \\
    \tau \sum_{i=0}^{n-1} \lr{\Gamma\,\gamma_{ads}(k) - \con{\ammonia}^{ads}(k{+}i\tau)\,\gamma_{des}(k)}
\end{multline}
where $\gamma_{ads} = k_{ads}\,\con{\ammonia}^{in}$ and
$\gamma_{des} = k_{ads}\,\con{\ammonia}^{in} + k_{des} + k_{scr}\,\con{\nox}^{in} + k_{oxi}$.

The sum over the unknown sub-step values $\con{\ammonia}^{ads}(k{+}i\tau)$ defines the \textit{average surface
concentration} within the sample:
\begin{align}
    \sigma(k) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\con{\ammonia}^{ads}(k + i\tau)
\end{align}
This $\sigma(k)$ is unobservable. We cannot measure the surface concentration at each sub-step.

\itbf{$A_7$:} Zero-order hold for the average surface concentration: $\sigma(k) \approx \con{\ammonia}^{ads}(k)$,
i.e., the surface concentration at the start of the sample dominates the average. Using $n\tau = t_s$:
\begin{multline}
    \sigma^{ub}(k{+}1) = \sigma(k) + t_s\,k_{ads}\,\con{\ammonia}^{in}(k)\lr{\Gamma - \sigma(k)} \\
        - t_s\,k_{od}\,\sigma(k) - t_s\,k_{scr}\,u_1(k)\,\sigma(k)
    \label{eqn::sigma_dynamics}
\end{multline}
Equation~(\ref{eqn::sigma_dynamics}) assumes the catalyst remains unsaturated. In practice, the surface can saturate
($\sigma = \Gamma$) or deplete ($\sigma = 0$). This is enforced by a switching function applied to the unbounded update
$\sigma^{ub}$:
\begin{align}
    g_{\sat}(\sigma) &= \begin{cases}
        \sigma  & \text{if } 0 \leq \sigma \leq \Gamma \\
        \Gamma  & \text{if } \sigma > \Gamma \\
        0       & \text{if } \sigma < 0
    \end{cases}
    \label{eqn::g_sat} \\
    \sigma(k) &= g_{\sat}\lr{\sigma^{ub}(k)}
    \label{eqn::sigma_switched}
\end{align}

\subsubsection{$\nox$ Output}

Unlike adsorbed $\ammonia$, $\nox$ has no storage on the surface. The outlet concentration at each residence time
depends only on the inlet concentration and the surface state during that sub-step:
\begin{multline}
    \con{\nox}^{out}(k{+}(i{+}1)\tau) = \con{\nox}^{in}(k{+}i\tau) \\
        - \tau\,k_{s2v}\,k_{scr}\,\con{\ammonia}^{ads}(k{+}i\tau)\,\con{\nox}^{in}(k{+}i\tau)
\end{multline}
There is no telescopic accumulation. The measured outlet $\nox$ corresponds to the last residence time ($i = n{-}1$).
Applying ZOH for inlet $\nox$ ($u_1(k)$) and the same surface approximation ($\sigma(k)$):
\begin{align}
    x_1(k{+}1) = u_1(k)\lr{1 - \tau\,k_{s2v}\,k_{scr}\,\sigma(k)}
    \label{eqn::nox_output}
\end{align}

\subsubsection{Urea Dosing}

The injected urea solution (AdBlue) undergoes evaporation, thermal decomposition, and isocyanic acid hydrolysis. These
three steps aggregate to:
\begin{align*}
    \urea \longrightarrow 2\,\ammonia + \cotwo + x\,\water
\end{align*}
Since the urea concentration in AdBlue is fixed, the decomposition rate $r_u = k_u \con{\urea}$ is a constant. It
depends only on the volume of solution injected, not on temperature (the injector preheats the solution). The total
moles of $\ammonia$ produced in one sampling period from injected volume $t_s\,u_2(k)$:
\begin{align}
    \mol{\ammonia}^{in}(k) = \underbrace{2\,r_u}_{\text{rate}}
        \times \underbrace{t_s\,u_2(k)}_{\text{volume injected}}
        \times \underbrace{t_s}_{\text{duration}}
\end{align}
These moles are distributed evenly over the $n$ residence times. Dividing by the chamber volume $V$ and substituting
$n = t_s/\tau$:
\begin{align}
    \con{\ammonia}^{in}(k) = \frac{\mol{\ammonia}^{in}(k)}{nV}
        = \frac{2\,r_u\,t_s\,\tau}{V}\;u_2(k)
\end{align}
Substituting $\tau = V\rho_0/F(k)$ and defining $\nu_u = 2\,r_u\,t_s\,\rho_0$:
\begin{align}
    \con{\ammonia}^{in}(k) = \nu_u\,\frac{u_2(k)}{F(k)}
    \label{eqn::urea_dosing}
\end{align}
The inlet $\ammonia$ concentration is inversely proportional to flow. At lower flow, the same injection rate produces a
higher concentration in the chamber. This reciprocal relationship breaks down at zero flow, which is physically
meaningful (no exhaust to carry the reactant).
